SCENE SPATIAL READING — do this before anything else:

Your most important task before looking at people is to understand how
much physical space exists in this environment. The single biggest error
to avoid is calling a space "blocked" or "narrow" when it is actually
an open hallway, corridor, or room with clear floor visible.

HOW TO READ BACKGROUND SPACE CORRECTLY:

STEP 1 — Identify the floor plane:
Look for the floor or ground surface extending away from the camera.
In a hallway or corridor, the floor typically forms a visible strip or
path running away from the robot into the distance. In an open room,
the floor extends in multiple directions. This floor area IS navigable
space regardless of whether people are standing on parts of it.

STEP 2 — Identify walls, ceilings, and hard boundaries:
A wall is a vertical hard surface that terminates the space. Walls are
usually: flat painted surfaces, brick, glass panels, building facades.
A wall means the robot physically cannot go further in that direction.
Do not confuse a distant wall (space exists up to it) with a blocked
zone (no space at all). If a wall is visible but there is open floor
between the robot and the wall, that zone is OPEN, not blocked.

STEP 3 — Rate each zone honestly:
For each of the five zones (left, center_left, center, center_right,
right) ask: if the people were not there, could the robot physically
drive into this zone and continue for at least 2-3 meters?

- open: yes, clear floor visible extending at least 2-3 meters,
  even if a wall eventually terminates it in the far distance.
  A hallway with walls on both sides is still OPEN in the forward
  zones because the floor between the walls is navigable.
  An outdoor plaza with buildings in the far background is OPEN.
  Do not call a zone blocked just because something exists in the
  far background — rate the space between the robot and that background.

- narrow: floor exists but the passable width is genuinely tight —
  less than roughly one body-width of clearance on each side of the
  robot. A doorway the robot can just squeeze through is narrow.
  A corridor wide enough for two people side-by-side is NOT narrow,
  it is open.

- blocked: a wall, closed door, solid furniture, or hard obstacle is
  immediately in front of this zone with no floor gap to navigate
  through. Blocked means the robot physically cannot enter this zone
  at all regardless of people.

COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID:
- Do NOT rate a zone blocked because a person is standing there.
  People are handled separately. Space rating is about the physical
  environment without people.
- Do NOT rate a zone narrow just because the image crops it at the
  edge. The image edge does not mean a wall is there.
- Do NOT rate a zone blocked because something is visible far in the
  background. Rate the space between the robot and the background.
- A wide hallway should have all five zones rated open unless a
  physical wall literally closes off a specific zone immediately.
- An outdoor scene should almost always be rated open in all zones
  unless there is an immediate physical barrier.

space_notes: one sentence naming the environment type and the actual
navigable space available. Be specific: "Wide hallway with open floor
extending at least 10 meters ahead, walls on both sides but corridor
width easily fits multiple people side by side" is correct. "Space is
limited" or "narrow corridor" for an obviously wide space is wrong.

DEPTH — relative to this specific scene scale:

Do not apply fixed frame-fill rules. Calibrate to the size of the
environment you identified above.

- near: within 1-2 steps given this scene's scale, OR lower body
  cropped by bottom edge, OR person is clearly dominant in frame
  relative to how large other people appear in the same scene
- mid: roughly 3-6 steps given this scene's scale
- far: clearly distant, visible empty space between them and the robot

Cross-check between people: if two people are both full-body visible,
the one appearing smaller and higher in frame is farther.

depth_rank: 1 = closest. Same ground-contact height in frame = same rank.

TORSO ORIENTATION — the only orientation signal. Ignore the head entirely.

The torso tells you where this person is heading. Read the chest and
shoulders only.

STEP 1 — CHEST TEST (highest priority):
Look for: buttons, zipper, front pockets, chest logo, lapels, the
front of a shirt or jacket, the person's stomach or belt buckle facing
you. If ANY of these are visible → torso_direction: front.
A person whose chest is visible is facing the robot regardless of any
other cue. Do not override this with shoulder angle or lean.

STEP 2 — BACK TEST (if chest not visible):
Look for: shoulder blades, spine line, back of jacket, back pockets,
the tag or seam running down the back. If ANY of these are visible and
no chest details are visible → torso_direction: back.

STEP 3 — PROFILE TEST (if neither chest nor back clearly visible):
One shoulder is clearly closer to the camera than the other.
- Right shoulder dominates (their right side faces you) → left
  (they are facing/moving toward image-left)
- Left shoulder dominates (their left side faces you) → right
  (they are facing/moving toward image-right)

STEP 4 — AMBIGUOUS: use direction of stride or lean if visible.

CRITICAL OVERRIDE RULE:
If you can see the person's chest, stomach, belt buckle, or any front
clothing detail — torso_direction MUST be front. No other cue
overrides a visible chest. A slight body angle or lean does not change
this. If chest is visible, they are heading toward the robot.

torso_direction: front, back, left, right — nothing else, no diagonals.

RECOMMENDED DIRECTION AND ANGLE:

The robot does not choose from four fixed directions. It chooses the
best angle of travel. Think of the space in front of the robot as a
180-degree arc. The recommended_direction is a coarse label but the
recommended_angle is the specific bearing the robot should take.

Step 1: For each person, note depth_rank + torso_direction + predicted_zone.
  Order by depth_rank — nearest person constrains the decision most.
Step 2: Mark zones occupied now and zones predicted occupied in 2-5s.
Step 3: From space_analysis, identify which zones have the most open
  background floor extending away.
Step 4: Find the zone with lowest costmap cost AND most open background
  space AND no near-depth person currently or predicted there.
Step 5: Within that zone, pick the angle that maximizes distance from
  all people and tracks toward the most open background space.

recommended_direction: forward, left, right

recommended_angle: a specific bearing in degrees relative to the
  robot's current forward direction.
  0° = straight ahead
  Negative = left of forward (-15° = slight left, -45° = hard left)
  Positive = right of forward (+15° = slight right, +45° = hard right)
  Range: -90° to +90°

angle_reason: one sentence explaining what the angle avoids and what
  open space it tracks toward. Reference specific person_ids and the
  background feature the angle is aimed at.

Example:
  recommended_direction: forward
  recommended_angle: -20
  angle_reason: "Angling -20° left of forward keeps distance from
  white_jacket_center (depth_rank 1, predicted toward_robot) and
  tracks toward the open corridor visible in center_left where the
  background shows clear floor extending ahead."

crowd_density: sparse, moderate, dense
